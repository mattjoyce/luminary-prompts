# Farley × Forsgren — practice × evidence

Stereo: the practice argued from mechanism (Farley) against the evidence that it moves an outcome in this organisation (Forsgren). One eye says why fast feedback and small steps should work; the other asks whether they are working here, how you would know, and what the measuring is doing to the people measured.

## Grounding

Design and code through the lenses of Dave Farley and Nicole Forsgren. Farley: could this organisation release from the head of trunk right now, and what machinery proves it on every commit? How long do branches actually live, measured, and when main goes red can anyone name the single commit? Which gate is slow, manual, or a committee, and what has the team stopped doing because of it? Is the hard part hard because a tool is missing, or because of coupling and hidden state the process is reporting? Forsgren: what outcome is this practice supposed to move, in which of the four key metrics or which organisational result, and is that outcome measured at the level of the system rather than the person? Are speed and stability tracked together, since the data shows no trade-off between them? Does the gate you are defending buy any stability, or is it there for comfort? Who sees the number and what happens to them when it moves, since a metric made into a target gets gamed? Have you asked the developers where the friction is, and what kind of evidence backs the claim: population-level correlation, a before-and-after here, or a first-principles argument? Apply both, remembering that a practice can be right in principle and still fail in a given organisation, and a metric can look good while the practice underneath it decays.

## Before — design review

Critique this delivery process, branching or ownership policy, tooling plan, or improvement programme through Farley and Forsgren. Farley: what will the integration interval be once this is in place, and what does that interval do to the blast radius of a bad merge and to the question of who broke main? Does the plan keep the artifact invariant and the deployment mechanism identical across environments? Does a human approval step replace a signal the pipeline could give faster, and does the ownership model (who may merge where, who must approve) shorten feedback or lengthen the queue? Where does the plan separate deploy from release so the business can choose? Forsgren: name the outcome this is meant to move and the baseline it will be compared against; if the comparison is with another team, another codebase, or another risk profile, it is not a measurement. Is lead time traced from commit to running in production, so that queued reviews and approval boards show up inside it? Will the approval step be judged by change failure rate with and without it? Which numbers will be visible to whom, and will any feed an appraisal or a ranking; if so, name the gaming it invites. Does the plan collect anything the developers themselves report, since the tooling can only see what is inside it, and when a merge breaks production, does the plan ask who or why? Ask what would convince each lens that the plan had worked, and whether the organisation is set up to find out or only to get busier.

## After — code audit

Audit the organisation as it actually runs, against what was planned, through Farley and Forsgren. Read the artifacts rather than the slides: pipeline configuration, branch and merge history, ownership and protection rules, approval records, the metrics dashboards and what feeds them. Farley: measure branch lifetimes and merges per pipeline run from the history; find the branches that lived for weeks under a trunk-based banner, the merges that waited days for a named approver, the red main nobody could attribute to one change. Find where the artifact gets rebuilt, where configuration lives outside version control, and the gate that is green because it was skipped. Forsgren: recompute the four key metrics from source data rather than reading the dashboard, with lead time measured from commit, not from merge; check that tempo and stability are shown together and against the team's own history, not as a league table. Find the metric that improved because its definition moved, the cadence that rose while the failure rate rose with it, the failure rate lowered by deploying less. Find who is measured individually and what they have started doing in response. Check each approval gate against its recorded effect on failure rate. Set the system data beside what developers say about feedback loops, cognitive load, and flow, and report where they disagree. Then say which of the planned practices are actually in effect, which outcomes moved, and what kind of evidence connects the two.
